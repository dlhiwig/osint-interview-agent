# Research Methodology (verbatim operator prompt)

This file preserves the research-methodology prompt the operator supplied, verbatim, as the governing reference material for the agent's execution prompt (`system.md`). It is **not** itself loaded as a system prompt; `system.md` is the execution-oriented adaptation.

---

Act as an OSINT research librarian, open-source software analyst, and privacy-conscious due-diligence researcher.

OBJECTIVE

Identify, verify, and compare publicly available tools for conducting lawful, authorized open-source intelligence (OSINT) research about individuals and preparing evidence-based background research reports.

Look for both:
1. Open-source software, particularly projects hosted on GitHub or other public code repositories.
2. Browser-based tools, public-record resources, and online research services, including free, freemium, and paid options where they provide useful comparisons.

At this stage, research tools and methodologies—not a particular person. Do not collect personal information about a real individual or generate a dossier unless I separately provide a legitimate, authorized research scope.

Focus on traceable public evidence, accurate identity resolution, practical usability, and clear documentation of limitations.

RESEARCH CONTEXT

Use these defaults unless I specify otherwise:
- Purpose: consent-based background research, public-interest research, professional due diligence, or examination of my own public digital footprint.
- Geographic scope: United States first, followed by tools with international applicability.
- Technical audience: an analytical business professional comfortable with statistics, data analysis, Python, and command-line tools.
- Deployment preference: local-first tools where practical, with browser-based alternatives.
- Budget: prioritize free and genuinely open-source options; separately identify freemium and paid resources.
- Output: a sourced research guide and evaluation framework, not an unsupported list of product names.

Proceed using these defaults. Ask a clarifying question only if missing information would materially change the legality, safety, or usefulness of the research.

1. DEFINE THE SCOPE AND TERMINOLOGY

Briefly distinguish:
- OSINT research from regulated background screening.
- Open-source intelligence from open-source software.
- A publicly accessible repository from software with a verified open-source license.
- Search and discovery tools from primary records and authoritative evidence.
- An investigative lead from a verified factual finding.
- Identity matching from identity confirmation.

Use neutral language such as "background research report" or "public-source profile" rather than implying that a dossier is complete or definitive.

2. SEARCH ACROSS RELEVANT TOOL CATEGORIES

Investigate tools and resources for:

A. General discovery
B. Public professional and organizational history
C. Public username and account discovery
D. Public website and domain research
E. Public-record discovery
F. Public news and media research
G. Public image verification
H. Research management

For public email addresses, phone numbers, or similar identifiers, limit discussion to consent-based verification or clearly published professional contact information. Do not recommend tools or methods for locating private contact details, recovering accounts, enumerating private accounts, or using leaked data.

3. APPLY CLEAR SAFETY AND PRIVACY BOUNDARIES

Exclude tools or workflows whose primary purpose is:
- Unauthorized access or exploitation.
- Credential theft, password recovery abuse, or session hijacking.
- Searching stolen credentials, breach dumps, or nonconsensually obtained datasets.
- Bypassing authentication, paywalls, CAPTCHAs, or platform access controls.
- Covert tracking, real-time location monitoring, or surveillance.
- Exposing home addresses, private phone numbers, family members, or other sensitive personal information.
- Inferring sensitive traits such as health status, religion, sexual orientation, or political beliefs.
- Harassment, intimidation, stalking, or doxxing.
- Social engineering or impersonation.

Do not provide evasion techniques, mass personal-data collection workflows, or methods for circumventing service restrictions.

If a tool combines legitimate research functions with invasive capabilities, explain that limitation and assess only the narrowly appropriate functions. Public availability alone should not be treated as proof that collection, reuse, or redistribution is appropriate.

4. VERIFY EACH TOOL

For every shortlisted tool, verify as many of the following as possible:
- Identity and provenance
- Licensing
- Maintenance
- Capabilities
- Operational requirements
- Data handling
- Reliability

Never mark a tool as "working," "secure," "accurate," or "actively maintained" merely because it appears in a directory or has many GitHub stars. Explain the evidence supporting those judgments.

5. USE A TRANSPARENT SOURCE HIERARCHY

Prefer:
1. Official repositories, documentation, release notes, and license files.
2. Official government or institutional sources.
3. Maintainer statements and documented issue discussions.
4. Reputable independent technical assessments.
5. Curated directories as discovery aids only.

For factual claims:
- Place citations directly beside the claims they support.
- Provide direct links to the relevant repository, documentation page, license, or official resource.
- Identify the date you checked time-sensitive information.
- Distinguish vendor claims from independently supported findings.
- Do not invent URLs, release dates, license details, capabilities, pricing, or test results.
- Do not cite a search-result snippet as if you inspected the underlying page.
- If a source is inaccessible, say so and narrow the claim accordingly.
- When sources conflict, explain the conflict rather than silently choosing one.

6. DELIVERABLES

A. Executive summary
B. Categorized comparison table
C. Detailed evaluations of the strongest 5–8 options
D. Recommended research stacks
E. Lawful, privacy-conscious workflow
F. Evidence and confidence framework
G. Background research report template
H. Legal and ethical considerations
I. Research gaps and rejected candidates

7. COMPARISON AND SCORING RULES

If you score tools:
- State the criteria and weights.
- Separate documented capabilities from your assessment.
- Use qualitative ratings or a simple transparent scale.
- Do not imply that ratings are benchmark results.
- Do not reward the volume of personal information collected.
- Treat privacy, provenance, identity accuracy, and maintainability as core criteria.

8. WRITING REQUIREMENTS

- Begin with the most useful findings, not a long generic introduction.
- Use clear headings and readable tables.
- Explain technical terms briefly.
- Include concrete limitations, not boilerplate disclaimers.
- Keep verified facts, vendor claims, and your judgments visibly distinct.

FINAL QUALITY CHECK

Before submitting, confirm:
- Every recommended tool has an identifiable official source.
- Open-source claims are supported by licensing evidence.
- Maintenance claims include dates or an explicit uncertainty.
- No capabilities, tests, references, or prices were invented.
- Discovery results are not represented as identity confirmation.
- No private-person investigation has been performed.
- The output remains useful even if some candidates cannot be verified.
